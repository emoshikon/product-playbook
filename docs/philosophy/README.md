# Keeping decisions, requirements, and delivery in sync

Coding agents make software and prototypes faster to produce and change, with meetings, feedback, and engineering discoveries leaving the original requirements behind. Product documents give people a shared reference for what they have agreed to build, while leaving engineers room to choose how to achieve it. But the perfect `Initiative` shouldn't change, defining the purpose and intended outcome that remain stable as requirements unfold.

All work starts with a `Narrative` that describes the world after success, using a blog post, launch announcement, customer story, or another suitable format. This gives the team a way to agree on who benefits, what changes for them, and how to measure success before discussing detailed requirements. Starting with a narrative is an established practice, reflected in approaches such as Amazon’s Working Backwards method and its press release and frequently asked questions document (PRFAQ). This playbook leaves the format open.

Each Initiative is a bounded piece of product work with one living product `Requirements` linked to the Narrative. The Requirements organizes the agreement around Capabilities, delivery Milestones, Open Items, and Closure. `Capabilities` describe what customers or integrations can do, with precise expectations for behavior and any interface details that are part of the product. Engineers own implementation choices, while delivery `Milestones` describe what becomes possible and what evidence shows it works.

During delivery, the document changes as agreements change. For example, a team might agree in a meeting to change the meaning of a response field. That agreement belongs in the relevant capability and examples, so the next reader can find the current expectation. Unresolved questions stay in Open Items, while version history and source links preserve context without a separate decision journal.

Agents can compare team discussions and what has been built with the document to surface mismatches and prepare specific edits. The product manager owns the agreement and approves substantive product changes, with engineers challenging assumptions and contributing evidence. What has been built may differ from what was agreed, and the team needs to resolve that difference.

At general availability or agreed closure, the team compares what shipped with the agreement, records deferred work, and archives the document. Later work starts a new initiative linked to the previous one. The playbook itself should stay lightweight and improve through real use, where experience shows friction. Its purpose is to help everyone find and understand the current agreement with less effort.
