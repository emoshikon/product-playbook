# Keeping decisions, requirements, and delivery in sync

Coding agents make software and prototypes faster to produce and change, with meetings, feedback, and engineering discoveries leaving the original requirements behind. Product documents give people a shared reference for what they have agreed to build, while leaving engineers room to choose how to achieve it. An `Initiative` provides lasting purpose and intended outcome as requirements unfold. Its narrative can be revised when that intent changes, but should still hold when the team finds a different way to achieve the outcome.

All work starts with a `Narrative` that describes the world after success, using a blog post, launch announcement, customer story, or another suitable format. This gives the team a way to agree on who benefits, what changes for them, and how to measure success before discussing detailed requirements. Starting with a narrative is an established practice, reflected in approaches such as Amazon’s Working Backwards method and its press release and frequently asked questions document (PRFAQ). This playbook leaves the format open.

Each Initiative is a bounded piece of product work with a Narrative and one living `Requirements` document. The Requirements link to the Narrative and organize the agreement around Capabilities, delivery Milestones, Open Items, and Closure. `Capabilities` describe what customers or integrations can do, with precise expectations for behavior and any interface details that are part of the product. Engineers own implementation choices, while delivery `Milestones` describe what becomes possible and what evidence shows it works.

The Narrative and Requirements are the two canonical documents, supported by linked artifacts such as mockups, API examples, schemas, or other suitable definitions. Requirements and agreed supporting definitions specify the product together. Fine details can remain in those artifacts without being copied into requirements prose. Links should make each artifact's role and agreement status understandable.

During delivery, the document changes as agreements change. For example, a team might agree in a meeting to change the meaning of a response field. That agreement belongs in the relevant capability and examples, so the next reader can find the current expectation. Unresolved questions stay in Open Items, while version history and source links preserve context without a separate decision journal.

Agents compare team discussions and delivery evidence with both documents and affected supporting artifacts to surface mismatches and prepare coordinated edits. The product owner decides substantive changes to requirements, supporting definitions, and narrative intent, with engineers challenging assumptions and contributing evidence. Evolving requirements do not automatically justify rewriting the narrative. What has been built may differ from what was agreed, and the team needs to resolve that difference.

At general availability or agreed closure, the team compares delivery with requirements and narrative success. It records deferred work and unknown outcomes, then archives the requirements after confirmation. Shipping capabilities does not by itself prove the intended outcome occurred. Later work starts a new initiative linked to the previous one. The playbook itself should stay lightweight and improve through real use, where experience shows friction. Its purpose is to help everyone find and understand the current agreement with less effort.
